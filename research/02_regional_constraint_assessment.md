# 02 Regional Constraint Mapping

**Last Updated:** 2026-07-19 
**Status:** Completed  
**Primary Regions:** PJM, ERCOT, CAISO  
**Module Progress:** 100%

## Executive Conclusion

Power availability constraints affecting AI infrastructure are regional rather than uniform across the United States. The evidence does not support treating national generation capacity, reserve margins, or announced data center demand as sufficient indicators of deployment feasibility.

Among the three regions examined, PJM currently presents the strongest evidence of an active and geographically concentrated deployment constraint. The constraint is most visible in the Dominion zone, including Northern Virginia, where rapid data center-driven load growth is interacting with transmission deliverability, local infrastructure requirements, resource adequacy, and the accuracy of large-load forecasts.

ERCOT presents the largest potential headline increase in large-load demand, but the requested-load figures substantially exceed the demand that ERCOT currently treats as likely to materialize. The region may provide greater operational flexibility than PJM, but this flexibility can involve curtailment exposure, conditional service, project-delay assumptions, and uncertainty over which projects will reach commercial operation.

CAISO is experiencing increasing large-load applications and has begun formal policy and technical work concerning their integration. However, currently published data center load forecasts indicate a smaller near-term increase than the principal PJM and ERCOT growth centers. CAISO therefore functions primarily as a planning and policy comparison within this module rather than as the strongest current example of an AI infrastructure power constraint.

The module’s central finding is that **transmission deliverability, utility service readiness, project credibility, and interconnection timing are more decision-useful indicators of deployment risk than aggregate regional generation capacity alone**.

---

## 1. Research Objective

Module 1 established that power availability is a credible constraint on the expansion of AI infrastructure. Module 2 identifies where that constraint is most likely to affect project deployment.

The objective is not to determine which electricity market is universally “best” for data center development. Such a ranking would be misleading because regions differ in market structure, load forecasting methods, transmission topology, generation mix, utility service procedures, project realization rates, and the treatment of flexible loads.

Instead, the module evaluates three questions:

1. Where is data center-related electricity demand already visible in official planning data?
2. Where does expected load growth appear most likely to encounter delivery, reliability, or interconnection constraints?
3. Which indicators should investors use to distinguish announced demand from serviceable and economically viable demand?

The analysis focuses on PJM, ERCOT, and CAISO as initial comparison regions.

## 3. Analytical Framework

The research separates power availability into six components.

### 3.1 Forecasted Demand

The first indicator is the amount and rate of anticipated load growth. However, forecasted load must be separated into:

- Existing operating demand
- Contractually committed demand
- Approved or advanced-stage service requests
- Early-stage applications
- Duplicate or competing site requests
- Flexible or curtailable demand
- Speculative demand without sufficient project evidence

Headline megawatt requests are therefore not treated as equivalent to bankable load.

### 3.2 Generation and Resource Adequacy

A region may have sufficient annual generation but still face inadequate dependable capacity during peak periods. Reserve-margin and capacity indicators remain relevant, but they do not independently establish whether electricity can be delivered to a specific data center site.

### 3.3 Transmission Deliverability

Transmission constraints determine whether existing or planned generation can reach a particular load pocket. This is especially important when new demand is geographically concentrated.

Transmission deliverability includes:

- Local and regional transmission capacity
- Required network upgrades
- Thermal or voltage violations
- Import dependence
- Timing of planned reinforcements
- Allocation of upgrade costs
- Exposure to project delays

### 3.4 Utility Service Readiness

ISO-level capacity does not guarantee that a local utility can connect and serve a facility on the required timeline. Utility service readiness includes substations, transformers, feeders, land, equipment procurement, permitting, and construction sequencing.

### 3.5 Operational Flexibility

Some large loads may accept curtailment, ramping limits, behind-the-meter generation, demand response participation, or staged energization. These arrangements can accelerate access to power but may reduce operating certainty.

### 3.6 Forecast and Project Credibility

Because multiple projects may compete for the same land, generation, transmission, equipment, and customers, the probability that a requested load will become operational must be incorporated into the regional comparison.

## 5. PJM Assessment

### 5.1 Demand Concentration

PJM forecasts substantial long-term demand growth, with data center-heavy zones contributing disproportionately to the regional increase.

Previous evidence collection found that PJM’s 2026 forecast implies approximately 65.7 GW of additional summer peak demand over ten years and significant variation among zones.

The most important concentration is the Dominion zone, which covers much of Virginia and includes the Northern Virginia data center market.

Virginia commercial electricity sales increased by nearly 30 million MWh between 2019 and 2025, with data centers identified as the principal growth driver. Dominion-zone summer peak demand reached 23,905 MW in 2025, while winter peak demand in the 2025–2026 season was materially above the 2019–2020 level. PJM expects Dominion summer peak demand to grow rapidly over the next ten years.

This evidence establishes that the PJM constraint is not merely a projection based on uncommitted applications. Material load growth is already visible in historical consumption and peak-demand data.

### 5.2 Nature of the Constraint

The evidence suggests that PJM’s problem should not be characterized solely as insufficient aggregate generation. The constraint includes at least four interacting elements:

- Rapid demand growth in specific load pockets
- Dependence on power transfers into high-growth areas
- Transmission reinforcements required to deliver power
- Resource adequacy pressure as large-load growth occurs alongside generation retirements and slow project development

The Dominion market illustrates why a regional grid can have adequate resources in aggregate while still presenting site-specific power-access constraints.

A data center project may therefore face delay even when PJM-wide nameplate generation appears sufficient. The relevant question is whether dependable power can be delivered to the requested location by the facility’s target energization date.

### 5.3 Forecast Reliability

PJM distinguishes between projects based on timing and supporting evidence. Near-term adjustments generally require firmer project commitments, while longer-term additions remain subject to greater uncertainty and derating.

This does not invalidate the PJM constraint thesis. It means the exact magnitude should be treated as a forecast range rather than a precise point estimate.

### 5.4 PJM Conclusion

PJM provides the strongest current evidence of a binding regional AI infrastructure constraint because:

1. Data center demand is geographically concentrated.
2. Historical electricity consumption and peak loads already demonstrate material growth.
3. The growth is affecting both transmission and resource-planning requirements.
4. The constraint is visible below the RTO level in the Dominion zone.
5. The issue concerns deliverability and infrastructure timing, not only forecasted energy consumption.

PJM should therefore receive the highest research priority in subsequent ownership and investment mapping.

## 6. ERCOT Assessment

### 6.1 Headline Growth

ERCOT has received exceptionally large volumes of prospective large-load demand. Previous evidence collection identified approximately 77,965 MW of data center growth included in transmission service provider projections for 2030 and a total unadjusted demand forecast reaching approximately 218 GW by 2031.

These values demonstrate the scale of developer interest in Texas, but they should not be interpreted as equivalent to firm electricity demand.

### 6.2 Forecast Adjustment

ERCOT adjusts large-load forecasts using observed project behavior. Its methodology accounts for:

- Delays relative to requested energization dates
- Historical realization rates
- Observed peak consumption relative to requested capacity
- Differences among large-load categories
- Updated information from transmission service providers

In the prior research, ERCOT’s methodology reduced new data center demand to approximately 49.8% of requested capacity and delayed assumed project ramps by approximately 180 days.

This adjustment is one of the most important findings of the module. It demonstrates that requested megawatts should not be compared directly across regions without accounting for forecasting methodology.

### 6.3 Nature of the Constraint

ERCOT’s constraint differs from PJM’s in three ways.

First, forecast uncertainty is more prominent. The gap between requested and adjusted load is large enough that project credibility becomes a central analytical variable.

Second, flexible or curtailable service may play a larger role. A data center capable of reducing consumption during system stress may obtain a different form of access from a continuously firm load.

Third, the region’s rapid development environment may allow some projects to secure power faster, but potentially under less certain operating conditions or with greater reliance on private generation, staged energization, batteries, demand response, or curtailment arrangements.

Therefore, ERCOT should not automatically be characterized as either less constrained or more constrained than PJM. It may be easier to connect certain projects, but the commercial quality of that power access can differ.

### 6.4 Curtailment as Both Solution and Risk

Curtailment can improve system flexibility by allowing large consumers to reduce demand when reserves are scarce or grid conditions become stressed.

For the developer, however, curtailable service may create:

- Reduced uptime certainty
- Backup generation and storage requirements
- Additional capital expenditure
- Operational complexity
- Exposure to market prices or grid conditions
- Constraints on service-level agreements with computing customers

Curtailment therefore does not eliminate the power constraint. It reallocates part of the constraint from the transmission system to the data center operator.

### 6.5 ERCOT Conclusion

ERCOT represents a high-growth but probability-weighted deployment market.

The available evidence supports the following conclusions:

1. Announced and requested demand is exceptionally large.
2. A material portion of that demand is not currently treated as certain to materialize.
3. Flexible-load mechanisms may permit faster development but can reduce power firmness.
4. The regional constraint must be evaluated project by project rather than through headline load forecasts.
5. The most useful ERCOT indicator is likely the conversion rate from requested capacity to adjusted forecast, executed interconnection, and actual consumption.

ERCOT remains a priority market, but its investment relevance depends on distinguishing projects with credible power arrangements from projects supported primarily by speculative service requests.

## 7. CAISO Assessment

### 7.1 Forecasted Growth

CAISO identifies data centers as the largest emerging large-load use case within its system. The California Energy Commission forecasts meaningful long-term data center load growth, while utilities are receiving an increasing number of large-load interconnection and service applications.

The forecast indicates meaningful long-term growth, but the quantified near-term increase is smaller than the principal growth signals observed in PJM and ERCOT.

### 7.2 Planning Structure

CAISO’s principal value to this module is its formal treatment of large-load planning.

The planning process involves several parties:

- California Energy Commission
- CAISO
- Transmission owners
- Utility distribution companies
- Load-serving entities
- Large-load customers

Large-load forecasts can incorporate utility service applications, confidence assessments, utilization assumptions, and staged ramp profiles rather than treating every request as fully operational from its initial requested date.

This provides a useful comparison with PJM’s commitment-based adjustments and ERCOT’s realization-based haircuts.

### 7.3 Technical and Policy Development

CAISO has begun formal work on planning roles, interconnection treatment, operating requirements, technical standards, and possible tariff changes related to large loads.

The existence of this process indicates that large-load integration is becoming material. It does not, by itself, establish that CAISO currently has a more severe physical constraint than PJM or ERCOT.

### 7.4 CAISO Conclusion

Current evidence supports treating CAISO as a planning and regulatory comparison rather than the primary near-term constraint case.

The region remains relevant because:

1. Large-load applications are increasing.
2. Data centers are the largest identified emerging large-load use case.
3. The state has a formal confidence-weighted forecasting process.
4. Technical and tariff standards for large loads remain under development.
5. Local concentration could still create utility-territory bottlenecks that are not visible in the regional forecast.

The principal unresolved issue is whether applications are concentrated within specific utility territories or transmission areas strongly enough to create local constraints before the broader CAISO system becomes capacity-constrained.

## 8. Standardized Regional Indicators

| Indicator | Definition | Investment Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Historical peak-load growth | Actual change in seasonal peak demand | Confirms whether forecast pressure is already materializing |
| Adjusted large-load forecast | Load remaining after probability, delay, and utilization adjustments | Separates requested demand from expected demand |
| Data center share of growth | Portion of regional or zonal growth attributable to data centers | Measures direct AI-infrastructure exposure |
| Geographic concentration | Share of load additions located within particular zones or utility territories | Identifies local bottleneck risk |
| Firm versus flexible load | Degree to which demand can be curtailed or staged | Measures quality and reliability of power access |
| Transmission upgrade requirement | Network investment needed to serve the load | Indicates timing, cost, and execution risk |
| Utility service timeline | Expected time for substation, transformer, and distribution work | More directly relevant to project delivery than regional capacity |
| Forecast revision rate | Change between successive forecast vintages | Measures forecast uncertainty |
| Requested-to-realized conversion | Percentage of requested MW reaching operation | Measures project credibility |
| Reserve and capacity position | Dependable supply relative to peak demand | Identifies broader adequacy exposure |
| Congestion and import dependence | Reliance on constrained transmission paths | Indicates deliverability and locational risk |
| Large-load application volume | Number and capacity of utility service requests | Early indicator requiring probability adjustment |

No single indicator should be used to rank regions. A high-growth region may remain deployable if transmission and generation are added rapidly, while a lower-growth region may experience severe local constraints if demand is concentrated in a weak part of the grid.

## 9. Final Regional Findings

### Finding 1: PJM Has the Strongest Demonstrated Constraint

PJM, particularly the Dominion zone, has the most complete combination of historical load growth, forecast concentration, transmission relevance, and planning pressure.

This finding is supported by observed demand rather than solely by announced projects.

### Finding 2: ERCOT Has the Largest Forecast-Credibility Problem

ERCOT’s headline large-load demand is not directly comparable with PJM or CAISO because ERCOT applies substantial adjustments for realization, delays, and actual consumption.

The central investment issue is not the total requested megawatts. It is the probability that a particular project secures sufficiently firm and timely power.

### Finding 3: CAISO Is a Useful Planning Control Case

CAISO currently presents weaker evidence of an immediate region-wide data center power bottleneck, but it provides a valuable model for studying how large-load applications, confidence levels, ramp assumptions, utility roles, and technical requirements are incorporated into formal planning.

### Finding 4: The Constraint Is Usually Local Before It Is Regional

The evidence supports evaluating the power constraint at the zone, utility territory, substation, and project level.

ISO-wide generation or reserve figures can obscure local transmission, transformer, and distribution limitations.

### Finding 5: Deliverability Is More Important Than Nameplate Capacity

Generation capacity that cannot connect, obtain permits, complete transmission upgrades, or deliver electricity into the required load pocket does not solve a data center’s deployment constraint.

### Finding 6: Requested Load Is Not Bankable Load

A service request represents developer interest, not necessarily a financed, permitted, contracted, and operational project.

Probability-weighted treatment is required across all regions, even where methodologies differ.

## 10. Investment Implications

The regional analysis changes the AI infrastructure investment thesis in several ways.

First, secured power should be treated as a differentiated asset rather than a standard input. Projects with existing utility service, advanced interconnection rights, energized substations, approved transmission upgrades, or credible behind-the-meter supply may command a premium over projects supported only by future power assumptions.

Second, the beneficiaries of AI-related load growth may differ by region. In PJM, transmission owners, regulated utilities, generation owners, and developers controlling suitable powered sites may be important. In ERCOT, flexible-load technology, private generation, storage, demand response, and developers capable of converting speculative applications into operating projects may matter more. In CAISO, utility planning, transmission development, technical compliance, and regulatory execution may be the more immediate channels.

Third, regional load growth should not automatically be treated as positive for every utility or infrastructure owner. Required capital spending can create growth opportunities, but it can also increase regulatory exposure, execution risk, customer-concentration risk, financing needs, and the possibility of stranded infrastructure if expected projects fail to materialize.

Fourth, data center developers with credible evidence of power control should be distinguished from those relying on nonbinding applications or distant planned upgrades.

## 11. Limitations

This module establishes a regional framework but does not provide a complete site-by-site deployment model.

Several limitations remain:

- Utility service-request data are often confidential or aggregated.
- Data centers may submit requests for multiple alternative sites.
- AI-specific demand is not consistently separated from cloud computing and conventional data center demand.
- ERCOT large-load categories may include crypto mining, industrial manufacturing, hydrogen, and other activities.
- Forecast methodologies differ substantially across regions.
- Transmission projects and load forecasts can change between planning cycles.
- Distribution-level constraints may not appear in ISO or RTO publications.
- A forecasted constraint does not prove that every project within the region will be delayed.
- Behind-the-meter generation and flexible-load arrangements can materially alter project outcomes.

These limitations reduce the precision of regional comparisons but do not overturn the conclusion that power access is regionally and locally differentiated.

---

## 12. Module Conclusion

Module 2 finds that power availability risk for AI infrastructure is best understood as a regional and subregional deliverability problem.

PJM currently provides the strongest demonstrated case, driven by concentrated demand in the Dominion and Northern Virginia market and its implications for transmission, resource adequacy, and utility infrastructure.

ERCOT provides the largest potential growth case but also the greatest need for probability adjustment. Its flexible-load framework may improve the speed or feasibility of some projects while transferring reliability and operating risk to the customer.

CAISO provides a lower-pressure comparison based on current published forecasts, while offering valuable evidence regarding planning coordination, application confidence, technical requirements, and long-term integration.

The evidence therefore supports the following research priority:

- **Primary ownership mapping:** PJM Dominion and Northern Virginia
- **Secondary ownership mapping:** ERCOT high-growth large-load areas
- **Planning and policy comparison:** CAISO utility and transmission framework

Module 3 should now examine **who owns, controls, finances, or benefits from the infrastructure required to resolve these constraints**, including regulated utilities, transmission owners, generation owners, equipment suppliers, data center developers, and entities controlling powered land or advanced interconnection positions.
